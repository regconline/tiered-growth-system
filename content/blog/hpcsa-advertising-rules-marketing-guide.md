Marketing a healthcare practice is different from marketing a restaurant or a hardware store. Practitioners registered with the Health Professions Council of South Africa (HPCSA) must follow ethical rules that govern how they advertise. The good news: those rules don't forbid marketing — they require it to be **honest, factual and respectful of patients**.

This guide explains the principles in plain English and how they apply to websites, social media and online ads.

> **Important:** this article is general guidance, not legal advice. Always check the current HPCSA ethical rules and guidance for your profession, and consult the HPCSA or a legal adviser if you're unsure.

## The core principle: factual, not misleading

The HPCSA's ethical rules allow practitioners to advertise their services, provided the advertising is not unprofessional, untruthful, deceptive or misleading, and doesn't cause unwarranted anxiety. In practice, ask three questions of any marketing material:

1. **Is it true and verifiable?**
2. **Could it mislead a reasonable patient?**
3. **Does it respect patients' dignity and the profession?**

If the answer to any of these is uncertain, rewrite it.

## What you generally *can* do

- Publish a website with your services, qualifications, registration, hours, location and fees policy.
- Run Google, Facebook or Instagram ads that factually describe your services.
- Share educational health content on social media and your blog.
- List which medical aid schemes you're contracted with.
- Show professional photos of your practice and team.
- Ask patients for honest feedback and reviews (without incentives).

## What to avoid

### Superlatives and comparisons

Claims like "the best dentist in Pretoria", "Cape Town's leading physio" or "better results than other clinics" are difficult to verify and imply superiority over colleagues. Describe what you do instead: "General and family dentistry in Pretoria East."

### Guaranteed outcomes

"Pain-free", "100% success", "results guaranteed" or "cure your back pain" promise outcomes that no practitioner can guarantee for every patient. Use language like "aims to", "may help" and "we'll discuss realistic outcomes with you".

### Testimonials and endorsements

Using patient testimonials to promote your services is a high-risk area in healthcare advertising. Avoid publishing testimonials on your website or ads, and never write or edit reviews on a patient's behalf. Reviews that patients leave independently on platforms like Google are different — you can reply to them professionally (without confirming anyone is a patient).

### Before-and-after images

Before-and-after photos can create unrealistic expectations and raise consent and confidentiality concerns. If your profession's guidance allows any use at all, you'd need explicit written consent and careful, non-misleading presentation. Most practices are safer without them.

### Fear-based messaging

"Don't wait until it's too late" campaigns that create unnecessary anxiety conflict with the spirit of the rules. Educate rather than alarm.

### Touting and canvassing

Approaching individuals directly to solicit them as patients — for example, unsolicited messages to people you don't have a relationship with — is not appropriate. Make yourself easy to find instead of chasing patients.

### Discounts that compromise care

Time-limited "specials" on clinical procedures can pressure patients into decisions. If you publish prices, make them accurate, complete and not designed to rush decisions.

## Applying the rules channel by channel

### Your website

- State facts: services, qualifications, registration numbers, location, fees policy.
- Write service pages that explain procedures honestly, including that outcomes vary.
- Add a privacy policy and POPIA-compliant forms (see our [POPIA guide](/blog/popia-compliance-medical-practice-website/)).

### Social media

- Share education, practice news, team introductions and health awareness days.
- Don't post identifiable patients without explicit written consent — and think twice even with consent.
- Don't give individual medical advice in comments; invite people to book a consultation.

Need ideas? See our list of [compliant social media post ideas](/blog/healthcare-social-media-content-ideas/).

### Google Ads and Meta ads

- Keep copy factual: service + location + call to action.
- Avoid superlatives and outcome promises in headlines.
- Note that advertising platforms have their own healthcare policies too.

### Reviews

- Ask for honest feedback; don't filter who you ask.
- Never offer gifts or discounts for reviews.
- Respond to every review professionally, never discussing a patient's care publicly.

## Compliant vs non-compliant examples

| Avoid | Prefer |
| --- | --- |
| "Joburg's #1 dentist" | "Family and cosmetic dentistry in Randburg" |
| "Pain-free implants guaranteed" | "We'll discuss implant options, comfort and recovery with you" |
| "Lose 10kg in 30 days" | "Personalised nutrition plans with a registered dietitian" |
| "Patients love us — read their stories!" | "Meet our team and learn about your first visit" |

## Other councils

Nurses (SANC), pharmacists (SAPC), allied health practitioners (AHPCSA) and other regulated professions have their own codes. The same principles — honesty, no misleading claims, respect for patients — run through all of them.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can doctors advertise in South Africa?**
Yes. Practitioners may advertise their services as long as the advertising is factual, not misleading and complies with the ethical rules of their council.

**Can I post patient reviews on my website?**
Using testimonials to promote your services is risky under healthcare advertising rules. It's safer to link to your independent Google reviews rather than republishing them as marketing.

**Can I use "specialist" in my marketing?**
Only if you are registered as a specialist in that field with the HPCSA. Misrepresenting qualifications is a serious breach.

**Can I advertise prices?**
You can publish fees, but they must be accurate and not misleading — include what's covered and what isn't.
