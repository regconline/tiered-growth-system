Marketing a psychology practice requires extra sensitivity. People searching for a therapist are often in a vulnerable place, confidentiality is paramount, and ethical rules apply. Done thoughtfully, though, good marketing simply helps the right clients find the right support sooner.

## Start with who you help

Clients don't search for "psychologist" alone — they search for help with something:

- Anxiety, depression or burnout.
- Grief and loss.
- Relationship and family difficulties.
- Child and adolescent support.
- Trauma.

Be clear about your areas of focus, modalities and who you're best placed to help. It's equally helpful to say who you're *not* the right fit for.

## A website that feels safe

Your website should feel calm, warm and easy to navigate:

- Plain, non-clinical language.
- A real photo and genuine bio — clients want to sense who you are.
- Clear information about session types (in-person, online), length, fees and medical aid.
- What to expect from a first session.
- A simple, low-pressure way to make contact.
- Crisis information: make it clear that you're not an emergency service and list crisis lines, such as the South African Depression and Anxiety Group (SADAG).

Keep contact forms minimal — name, contact details and preferred contact method. Don't ask people to describe their difficulties in detail through a web form.

## Be findable — discreetly

- Optimise your Google Business Profile with accurate categories and services.
- List your practice in reputable therapist directories and your professional association's directory.
- If you offer online sessions, say so clearly — it widens your reach across the country.

## Content that helps, not sells

Educational content builds trust and helps people decide to reach out:

- "What happens in a first therapy session?"
- "The difference between a psychologist, psychiatrist and counsellor."
- "Signs of burnout and when to seek help."
- Gentle wellbeing tips and coping strategies.

Avoid sensational headlines, promises of cure and anything that could cause distress.

## Social media with boundaries

Social media can reduce stigma and show your approach, but set clear boundaries:

- Share general psychoeducation, not individual advice.
- Never discuss clients, even anonymously, in ways that could identify them.
- Don't engage in therapeutic conversations in comments or DMs — guide people to book.
- Consider whether a professional page separate from your personal profile is right for you.

## Reviews and testimonials

Testimonials are particularly problematic in psychology: they can compromise confidentiality and create inappropriate dual relationships. Don't solicit testimonials. If clients leave independent reviews, respond in a general way that never confirms they are a client.

## Referral relationships

GPs, psychiatrists, schools, employee wellness programmes and other therapists are important referral sources. Let them know your focus areas, availability and how to refer.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can psychologists advertise in South Africa?**
Yes, within the ethical rules of the HPCSA. Advertising should be factual and not misleading, and must respect client confidentiality.

**Should I list my fees on my website?**
Many clients appreciate fee transparency, which reduces anxiety about reaching out. If you do, keep them accurate and current.

**Is it appropriate to run Google Ads for therapy?**
Yes, if ads are factual and compassionate. Advertising platforms may also restrict targeting based on sensitive health topics.
